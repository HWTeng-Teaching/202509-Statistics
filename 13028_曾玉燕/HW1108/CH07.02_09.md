A:  
$mean(\bar{x}) =\sum{\bar{x}p(\bar{x})}$  

without relacement
|$\bar{x}$|$p(\bar{x})$|$\bar{x}p(\bar{x})$|
|:-------:|:----------:|------------------:|
|12.5     |1/6         |2.083              |
|15.5     |1/6         |2.583              |
|16.0     |1/6         |2.667              |
|18.0     |1/6         |3.000              |
|18.5     |1/6         |3.083              |
|21.5     |1/6         |3.583              |
|         |sum         |17.000             |

with relacement
|$\bar{x}$|$p(\bar{x})$|$\bar{x}p(\bar{x})$|
|:-------:|:----------:|------------------:|
|10.0     |1/16        |0.625              |
|12.5     |2/16        |1.563              |
|15.0     |1/16        |0.938              |
|15.5     |2/16        |1.938              |
|16.0     |2/16        |2.000              |
|18.0     |2/16        |2.250              |
|18.5     |2/16        |2.313              |
|21.0     |1/16        |1.313              |
|21.5     |2/16        |2.688              |
|22.0     |1/16        |1.375              |
|         |sum         |17.000             |

Both sampling distributions have mean $E(\bar{X})=17$, which is the same as the population mean $\mu=17$. Thus, $\bar{X}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$ whether we sample with or without replacement.
